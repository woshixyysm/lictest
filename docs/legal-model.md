# Legal Model and Limitations

## 1. Purpose

This template is intended for a project whose actual rights holders want to make their own original code and documentation freely reusable by the public, including for commercial use, without attribution or share-alike conditions.

It combines:

- a separately signed rights-holder release / dedication record, inspired by public-domain dedication workflows such as SQLite's; and
- CC0 1.0 Universal, the Creative Commons public-domain dedication tool.

These instruments are complementary records. They are not two independent licenses and they cannot release rights the signer does not own.

## 2. What CC0 does

CC0 is a public-domain dedication tool. Its legal code first attempts a waiver of the affirmer's copyright and related rights to the greatest extent permitted by applicable law. If the waiver is ineffective, CC0 provides a broad fallback license and a further non-assertion commitment.

Canonical legal code: <https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/legalcode>

CC0 does not waive patents or trademarks held by the affirmer, and it does not clear rights belonging to other people. Some jurisdictions restrict waiver or licensing of moral rights and other rights.

## 3. Why keep a separate signed release?

A signed release can provide evidence of:

- who the rights holder is;
- what exact material is covered;
- the signer's ownership or authority;
- the signer's intention to dedicate the material to the public;
- the date and scope of the release;
- any excluded or separately licensed material.

A signed document does not make an invalid waiver valid everywhere. It also does not replace CC0's own legal code. Its effectiveness, formalities, and enforceability depend on applicable law.

## 4. Ownership must be resolved first

Copyright may belong to an employer, client, commissioning party, co-author, assignee, or other entity rather than the person who wrote the code. Joint works and work-made-for-hire rules differ by jurisdiction.

Before applying this template, determine:

1. who owns each contribution;
2. whether employment, contractor, university, grant, or client agreements affect ownership;
3. whether all co-owners must sign;
4. whether a company representative has authority to bind the company;
5. whether dependencies and copied material are excluded or separately cleared.

A Git author field, commit timestamp, repository ownership, or SPDX header is not proof of copyright ownership.

## 5. Rights that remain outside this model

This template does not automatically dispose of:

- patents or patent claims;
- trademarks, service marks, project names, or logos;
- privacy, publicity, confidentiality, trade-secret, or data-protection rights;
- non-waivable moral rights;
- third-party copyrights or database rights;
- contractual obligations owed to someone else.

Do not describe a project as free of all intellectual-property rights merely because its source code is marked CC0.

## 6. Suggested terminology

Prefer precise statements:

- "Dedicated under CC0 1.0 to the extent permitted by applicable law."
- "The named rights holder has signed the release for the identified files and commit."
- "Third-party materials are excluded and retain their own terms."

Avoid absolute claims such as "all rights worldwide are destroyed" or "no copyright exists anywhere." No template can guarantee that result in every jurisdiction.

## 7. Legal review

This is a general-purpose documentation template, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice. Have qualified counsel review it before use, especially where the project has multiple contributors, corporate ownership, employees, contractors, universities, patents, or contributors in several countries.
